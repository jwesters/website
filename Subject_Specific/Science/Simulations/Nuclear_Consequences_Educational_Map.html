Hiroshima — August 6, 1945

A uranium weapon with an estimated yield of roughly 15 kilotons was detonated over Hiroshima. Buildings, medical services, families, and neighbourhoods were devastated. Estimates vary, but approximately 140,000 people had died by the end of 1945. Survivors experienced burns, trauma, radiation illness, cancer risk, bereavement, displacement, and discrimination.

Nagasaki — August 9, 1945

A plutonium weapon with an estimated yield of roughly 21 kilotons was detonated over Nagasaki. Tens of thousands died in 1945, and many others lived with lifelong physical and psychological consequences. Terrain changed the pattern of destruction, illustrating why simple circles cannot predict real events.

Remembering people, not statistics

The people affected included children, patients, health workers, forced labourers, families, and entire communities. Numerical estimates cannot represent the individual lives, relationships, cultures, and futures lost.

Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)

Opened for signature in 1968 and in force since 1970, the NPT seeks to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons, support peaceful nuclear cooperation, and advance nuclear disarmament.

Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW)

Adopted in 2017 and in force since 2021, the TPNW establishes broad prohibitions on nuclear-weapon activities for participating states and recognizes assistance for victims and environmental remediation.

How this educational model works

This app uses broad circular zones that scale approximately with the cube root of explosive yield. The reference distances are intentionally simplified. Actual consequences depend on burst height, terrain, weather, construction, emergency preparedness, time of day, and many other factors.

It does not model fallout direction, weapon delivery, target value, optimal detonation height, or attack planning. Radiation and environmental effects are explained qualitatively because a trustworthy local estimate would require detailed atmospheric and geographic data.

Humanitarian estimates use a generic settlement-density range selected by the learner. They are not casualty predictions. The figures describe people potentially within broad disruption zones and people who might need temporary or long-term shelter.

Circle size can help learners understand scale, but it cannot express grief, disability, cultural loss, breakdown of health care, family separation, or decades of recovery.
